Tasiapik Valley, Umiujaq, Nunavik · 56.56° N, 76.48° W · four ground-temperature profiles, July 2014 to June 2018

Four patches of ground, one sky

Four ground-temperature profiles less than 200 m apart, under the same air. In the winter of 2014–15 the ground 10 cm down fell to at one site and only to at another. The difference is snow: shrubs, trees and hollows catch the snow the wind carries, and snow is a blanket. Below, a differentiable soil column driven by the measured weather and calibrated on these profiles by gradient-based least squares, shown against the sensors themselves. The last winter (2017–18) was held out of the fit.

How much snow, how much warming?

Mean annual ground temperature at 1 m, simulated for open lichen ground (the calibrated LLS soil and surface) under the weather of 2014–2024, repeated and shifted warmer or colder, for any amount of snow held. Below the line, the ground at 1 m averages below 0 °C, the threshold for permafrost. The four sites sit at zero shift, at their calibrated snow catch. In this sand, flowing groundwater (assumed to warm with the air) keeps everything below the water table above 0 °C whatever happens at the surface, which is why this valley's remaining permafrost is in ice-rich mounds rather than under these profiles (Dagenais et al. 2020).

How good is it, and what each piece buys

Root-mean-square error (°C) over all sensors. Calibration ("cal."): July 2014 to June 2017. Held out ("test"): July 2017 to June 2018 (no data at LH). "Prior" uses the literature values before fitting; the other columns refit the model without one piece of physics. Without groundwater the error rises at the two lichen sites, and the fit can only match the warm deep sand by making frozen sand conduct heat worse than thawed sand (ratio 0.70), which sand does not do. Without meltwater refreezing the overall error barely moves, but the spring error below 0.4 m grows by 10 to 15%: that term only acts during a few weeks a year.

What the model is

A one-dimensional heat equation with freezing and thawing (enthalpy form, 40 cells to 25 m, implicit daily steps solved by Newton), written in JAX so that every output is differentiable with respect to every coefficient. Top: sol-air temperature through a snow layer whose conductivity follows Sturm et al. (1997). Three pieces of physics were needed by the data: (1) snow catch per site, (2) meltwater that infiltrates frozen sand and refreezes, releasing latent heat (the spring zero curtain), with the refrozen ice tracked so it has to melt again, (3) exchange with flowing groundwater below the water table (the profiles are in piezometers in a sandy aquifer; Dagenais et al. 2020 show that heat carried by groundwater plays a critical role in permafrost thaw in this valley). Twenty-nine coefficients are fitted jointly by Levenberg–Marquardt with forward-mode Jacobians through 5,100 daily steps per site. The posterior is a Laplace approximation with the error variance inflated for the strong day-to-day correlation of the residuals, so the bands are honest about how few independent pieces of information four years of daily data contain. Sensor offsets (0.1–0.7 °C) were removed using the zero curtain as a reference; five faulty sensors were dropped.

Limits: the snow catch of a site is one number for all winters, while the wind redistributes snow differently each year (the 2014–15 winter is where the fit is worst); the groundwater temperature is a constant; the forcing before 2012 comes from a coastal station and ERA5, corrected to the valley station by monthly offsets.
